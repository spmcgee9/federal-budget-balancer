How these projections work

Starting point. CBO's February 2026 current-law projections for 2026–2056, which already reflect an aging population, rising health costs, interest rates, and economic growth.

The President's budget. CBO's June 2026 estimate of the President's 2027 spending proposals is added for 2027–2036. The budget proposed no tax changes. After 2036 the budget's lower discretionary spending is held at its 2036 share of GDP. Customs revenue is reduced to reflect CBO's July 2026 tariff update after the Supreme Court ended the IEEPA tariffs (about $250 billion in 2026, mostly refunds, and about $0.7 trillion over 2027–2036; CBO gave no year-by-year figures, so that amount is spread across years in proportion to projected customs revenue).

Your plan. For 2027–2031, your changes are applied to each account's year-by-year amounts in OMB's budget data, the same numbers as the Spending tab. After 2031, the change in each category (Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid and other health, other mandatory, discretionary) grows with CBO's projection for that category, so a 10% Social Security cut stays a 10% cut as the program grows.

Interest. CBO's projected interest, plus interest on any extra or avoided debt at CBO's projected average interest rate on federal debt (3.5% in 2027, rising to 4.2%).

Paying off the debt. If the debt isn't gone by 2056, it is projected further by holding 2056's revenue and spending shares of GDP, growth rate, and interest rate constant. That's an extrapolation, not a CBO projection.

Assumptions. The panel at the top can switch to OMB's economic forecast, change growth or interest rates, and turn on a feedback from debt to interest rates. Growth changes revenue in proportion and mandatory spending partly (0.4% per 1% of GDP); discretionary spending is set in dollars. Rate changes reach the average rate on the debt over about five years.

What's not modeled. Spending and tax changes don't change economic growth directly (beyond the optional interest-rate feedback). FY2027 differs slightly from the Spending tab because this tab uses CBO's estimates and the Spending tab uses OMB's. Independent analysts' totals for the President's budget vary with their assumptions.
